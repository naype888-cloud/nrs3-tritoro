NRS³·T³ · Bits on the boundary

Take a region of the three-torus. Every square on its walls is one bit: the step across it is open (1) or closed (0). Lean proves that the region can never hold more information than those bits, however deep it is. This is the Bekenstein bound, the picture of John Wheeler's "it from bit", proved here on T³. Only one way of filling the region reaches the top: every bit equally likely, nothing preferred. Nava's postulate says that a region filled to the top turns dark.

The walls of a region

The region is the slab on the left of the plane. On T³ every axis closes on itself, so the slab has two walls: the plane you choose, and the seam where the axis closes. Each wall has dy × dz squares. Orange: open (1). Empty: closed (0). Move the depth and the plane: the count of bits does not change. Drag to rotate.

With each square of area 4ℓ_P² log 2 (one bit of area, as on the open cube) the capacity is the Bekenstein–Hawking value A / 4ℓ_P² of the picture "bits on the boundary": area, not volume.

Fill it up

Now let every bit be 1 with the same chance. With a small chance the walls are almost all empty and hold little information; the information held grows as the chance goes to one half, and comes back down after. The bar reaches 100 % only at one half: every pattern equally likely. That is the only state that fills the region (Lean: the uniform distribution and no other).

The region turns dark only when it is full: that is Nava's postulate, declared in Lean as a bridge (HSaturationDark), not derived. Below the top the postulate says nothing, so the page does not shade the region in between.
